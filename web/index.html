What you actually do

Two things, and neither of them is steering. You assemble blueprints out of components — locomotion, armour, a manipulator, a radar, weapons — which compete for the same budget and add mass that slows the robot down. Then you write programs: an ordered list of rules, each one when these conditions hold, do this. Every robot re-decides every tick, and the first rule whose conditions match takes the tick. That list is your whole control surface.

A robot perceives a 90° wedge six cells deep in front of it, plus whatever radar you paid the mass for, plus two signals it can broadcast to its own colony across half the board and three coordinates it can remember. If it did not perceive a thing, no rule of yours can react to it. Two robots walking past each other facing the wrong way is not a bug; it is the game.

The simulation is server-side and authoritative: ten ticks a second on an arena generated from a seed nobody sees beforehand — 64×64 by default, and the host can pick anything from 32×32 to 128×128. Every colony starts on the same budget and keeps one base that cannot be destroyed — losing every robot is a setback rather than an elimination, because the base goes on building from whatever your scavengers brought home. Matches run to a fixed duration, ten minutes by default, whether or not anyone is watching.

The five pillars, one line each: autonomy first; program behaviour, not movement; modular trade-offs; emergent competition; readable simulation. They are §1.1 of the design document, which is what the game is trying to be; every question it left open was answered in the decisions log, together with the measurements behind the balance calls.

Your first match, in four steps

  1. Sign in with Google. Nothing but your identity is read from the account.
  2. Create a lobby on the lobbies page. The defaults make a fine first match: ten minutes, four seats.
  3. Add an AI opponent, and pick the rung you want. Tutorial and peaceful carry no weapon in any of their blueprints — a test pins that — so they compete with you for parts and cannot take a robot off the board at all. Defensive keeps armed responders and hurts a colony that blunders into it. Aggressive comes looking for you: measured against the default starter kit over sixteen seeds, it left that colony with no robots standing on ten of them.
  4. Start the match and watch. Click any robot for its loadout, its memory and the rule that made this tick's decision — and, on your own robots, to recall one or install a different program mid-match.

You do not have to write anything to play that first match: a colony that approves none of its own designs opens with a built-in starter kit. The kit is unarmed, and against the aggressive profile it usually dies — which is the moment to open the editor.

Where things are

  • Blueprint configurator — assemble the robot out of components, and see what each one costs you: the budget it spends, the speed the mass takes away, where it may no longer go, and which of your programs will still run on it.
  • Editor — the priority-rule programs those blueprints run. Checked against the robot as you edit, and a program can be dry-run against one before anything carries it into a match.
  • The rule language, in one page — what a robot can perceive, what each condition means, and three worked programs to start from.
  • Lobbies — create a match, seat AI colonies, wait for players, start it.
  • History — every match that has finished, with its final standing, its score graph, and the whole thing played back.
  • Source — Go and a deterministic simulation core; the client is this, plain HTML with no build step.
